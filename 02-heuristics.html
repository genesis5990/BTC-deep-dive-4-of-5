Tracing Heuristics

Addresses look random, so how do investigators group thousands of them into a handful of "wallets" or entities? Three classical heuristics — assumptions validated by how ordinary wallet software behaves.

Heuristic 1 — Common-input ownership

Rule: all addresses contributing inputs to the same transaction are assumed controlled by the same entity.

Why it usually works: to spend a UTXO you must produce its private key's signature. A typical wallet assembles enough of its own UTXOs to cover the payment — so the inputs almost always belong to one person.

Tx 9f2c…e1 (inputs)
 ├─ input 0  ← UTXO locked to address A1   ┐
 └─ input 1  ← UTXO locked to address A2   ┴─> assume same owner: cluster {A1, A2}

Where it breaks: collaborative transactions. CoinJoin-style constructions deliberately mix inputs from many strangers in one transaction. If a tx has 20+ inputs of equal size, treat this heuristic as unreliable and look for structure (equal denominations, peeling pattern) before clustering.

Heuristic 2 — Change detection

Rule: in a 2-output payment, one output is the payee, the other is change back to the sender. Spot the change and you have just linked a brand-new address to your subject.

Change output signals (strongest first):

  1. Address type match: modern wallets send change to the same script type they spent from (P2WPKH in → P2WPKH change). If the payment went to a different, newer type, the odd one out is often the payment.
  2. The "odd" address: if one output address appears in many transactions (reused — typical of a merchant/deposit address) and the other is brand new, the reused one is the payment and the fresh one is change.
  3. Round numbers: payments to people are often round (0.5 BTC, 0.25 BTC). Change is whatever awkward remainder makes the math work.
  4. Position: many wallets put change in a predictable output slot — useful only as a weak tiebreaker, never as primary proof.
  5. Amount: change is usually the smaller output — weak signal, use with others.
Tx 4bd1…7a :  inputs 1.20 BTC
 ├─ output 0: 0.50 BTC → bc1q…x9   (round amount, reused address)  => PAYMENT
 └─ output 1: 0.6997 BTC → bc1q…k3 (fresh, same type, remainder−fee) => CHANGE → now yours to follow

Heuristic 3 — Address reuse

Rule: an address that receives repeatedly (especially many small inflows from unrelated sources) is usually a deposit address of a service — exchange, payment processor, donation page — or a reused personal address.

Reuse lets you anchor a cluster: five different victims paying the same address in one week is a strong pattern to document. (Privacy best practice says never reuse; investigators benefit when people ignore best practice.)

Patterns to recognize on sight

Peel chainLong string of txs: each spends the previous change, sends a small payment forward, keeps peelingWallet paying many recipients, or a payout script; follow the change spine
ConsolidationMany inputs → one or two large outputsSubject sweeping funds (often before a large cash-out); big clustering win

The heuristics hierarchy of evidence

When you write your report, rank your findings: (1) cryptographic facts (signatures, amounts, timestamps — certain), (2) strong heuristic conclusions (multi-signal change detection — very likely), (3) single-signal guesses (position, "smaller output") — label them as inferences. Your case collapses if a defense expert shows you treated a guess as a fact.
Chain-hopping note for the room: today we trace on Bitcoin only. When value hops to another chain via a swap or bridge, the same UTXO logic ends at the hop — you continue with other techniques (covered in Class 5 preview).
